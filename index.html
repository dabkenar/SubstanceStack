    Half-life is how long your body takes to clear half the caffeine. It varies a lot between people, so if you know you're sensitive, pick a longer one.

    How the model works

    Caffeine is modeled as a one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination, the standard pharmacokinetic model for caffeine taken by mouth. Each drink is absorbed at a steady rate over the time it takes you to drink it, then every dose adds up.

    • Absorption: almost 100% of the caffeine you drink reaches your blood (Blanchard & Sawers, 1983). Liquid caffeine peaks about 40 minutes after you drink it, and coffee and cola peak at the same time (42 vs. 39 min). Capsules peak later, at about 67 min (Liguori et al., 1997). The model uses an absorption rate of 5.5/h for drinks and 2.9/h for pills, which reproduces those peaks. Sipping slowly spreads the dose out and flattens the peak.
    • Elimination: the half-life is about 5 hours in a typical healthy adult, but it ranges from about 1.5 to 9.5 hours. Smokers clear caffeine faster. Oral contraceptives roughly double the half-life, and late pregnancy can stretch it to about 15 hours (Nehlig, 2018; Institute of Medicine, 2001).
    • Sleep threshold: a meta-analysis of 24 studies found that a 107 mg coffee should be drunk at least 8.8 h before bed, and a 217.5 mg pre-workout at least 13.2 h before bed, to avoid losing sleep (Gardiner et al., 2023). At a 5 h half-life, both of those cutoffs leave about 30–35 mg in the body at bedtime, so the default threshold is 30 mg. 400 mg taken even 6 h before bed cut sleep by over an hour (Drake et al., 2013).
    • Body weight is used only to estimate blood concentration (caffeine spreads through about 0.7 L per kg of body weight).

    These are population averages, not medical advice. Your own response depends on genetics (the CYP1A2 enzyme), medications, liver health and tolerance. Caffeine amounts are typical values, so check your labels. Full references are in RESEARCH.md.
