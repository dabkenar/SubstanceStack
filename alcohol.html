    Your settings

    Clearance is how fast your liver removes alcohol. Most people clear about 0.015% BAC per hour, but it ranges from about 0.010% to 0.025%. If you're not sure, “Slower” gives the more cautious estimate.

    How the model works

    This uses the Widmark model, the standard way forensic scientists estimate blood alcohol, with gradual absorption from the stomach. Each drink is absorbed at a steady rate over the time it takes you to drink it, then your liver removes alcohol at a roughly constant rate.

    • How much alcohol: grams of alcohol = volume × ABV × 0.789 g/mL. One US standard drink is 14 g, about a 12 oz beer, a 5 oz glass of wine or a 1.5 oz shot (NIAAA).
    • Body water: alcohol spreads through the water in your body, so BAC = grams ÷ (r × body weight). The Widmark factor r is about 0.68 for men and 0.55 for women. “Prefer not to say” uses 0.55, the more cautious value.
    • Absorption: on an empty stomach, BAC peaks about 30–60 minutes after a drink. Eating lowers the peak considerably (Jones & Jönsson, 1994). The model only credits a meal with a 15% reduction and slower absorption, which is less than most studies find, to stay cautious.
    • Elimination: about 0.015% BAC per hour on average, ranging from about 0.010% to 0.025% in most people. Regular heavy drinkers clear alcohol faster (Jones, 2010). Coffee, water and sleep don't speed it up.
    • Driving: 0.08% is the legal limit in most of the US, but impairment starts much lower. At 0.02% judgment and the ability to track moving objects already decline, and at 0.05% coordination and response to emergencies are reduced (NHTSA).
    • Sleep: alcohol at bedtime helps you fall asleep faster, but it disrupts the second half of the night, and at moderate to high doses it suppresses REM sleep (Ebrahim et al., 2013). In 4,098 people, even low intake (≤0.25 g/kg) measurably reduced overnight recovery, and the effect grew with dose (Pietilä et al., 2018). That's why the default sleep target is 0.00%.

    These are population averages, not medical or legal advice. Your real BAC also depends on genetics, medications, liver health, how fast you drank and what you ate. Pours and ABVs vary, so check labels. Full references are in RESEARCH.md.
